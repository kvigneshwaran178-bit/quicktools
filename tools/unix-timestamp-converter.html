What Is a Unix Timestamp?

⏰

The Unix Epoch

A Unix timestamp counts the total seconds elapsed since January 1, 1970 at 00:00:00 UTC — known as the Unix Epoch. It is a universal, language-agnostic way to represent a specific moment in time.

⚙️

Why Developers Use It

Timestamps are storage-efficient, sortable, timezone-agnostic, and ideal for databases and APIs. Every major programming language has native support for parsing and generating them.

📏

Seconds vs Milliseconds

Standard Unix timestamps are in seconds (10 digits). JavaScript and many modern APIs use milliseconds (13 digits). This converter auto-detects and handles both formats transparently.

🌐

Timezone Safety

Timestamps represent a single moment in time regardless of timezone. The same timestamp displays different local times in Tokyo vs New York — but always refers to the same instant.

UTC vs Local Time

🌍

UTC (Coordinated Universal Time)

UTC is the primary time standard used by the internet and servers worldwide. It has no daylight saving offset, making it the most reliable reference point for logging events and syncing systems across timezones.

🏙️

Local Time

Local time is UTC adjusted by your timezone offset, including any DST rules. When displaying timestamps to end users, always convert to local time for clarity. Store internally as UTC to avoid confusion.

💡

Developer Best Practice

Store all timestamps as UTC in your database. Convert to local time only at the display layer, preferably client-side using the user's browser timezone. This eliminates entire classes of timezone bugs.
